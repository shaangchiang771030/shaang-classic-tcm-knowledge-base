Jing-Well, Ying-Spring, Shu-Stream, Jing-River, and He-Sea are among the oldest and most standardized groups of acupoints.

Traditionally, we memorize their names, Five-Phase assignments, and disease categories. But if the twelve channels really share a geometric template, another, more fundamental question can be asked:

**Why does every channel repeatedly install these five types of points near the limb extremities?**

If they were merely five convenient categories, their positions would not necessarily share a common structure. If they form a control system, similar phase hierarchies should recur across the twelve channels.

## Re-zero all twelve Jing-Well points

The most important correction in this round is to stop mixing raw absolute phases from different channels and instead set the Jing-Well point of each channel to 0.

This allows the distances of the Five-Shu points to be compared on one relative scale.

Using **cyclic phase distance from the Jing-Well point**, the median positions of the Five-Shu points across the twelve channels are approximately:

- Jing-Well: 0
- Ying-Spring: 0.0235
- Shu-Stream: 0.0419
- Jing-River: 0.0882
- He-Sea: 0.2266

These are not five evenly spaced points.

Instead, they resemble a control sequence unfolding layer by layer from an endpoint: Jing-Well is closest to the boundary; Ying-Spring, Shu-Stream, and Jing-River progressively move away from it; He-Sea enters a distinctly deeper longitudinal position.

## Jing-Well points resemble endpoint nodes

Among the twelve Jing-Well points, about **75%** have a best geometric role classified as a node.

This fits the classical sense of the “well” as the initial emergence or terminal boundary of channel qi. But the important point is not to prove the name from the name: even when looking only at geometry, Jing-Well points are indeed highly concentrated in endpoint/node-like roles.

They may therefore first be responsible for **boundary conditions**.

In a waveguide or oscillatory system, a boundary is not an ordinary location. Whether an endpoint is fixed, open, or reflective changes which modes the whole system can support.

This is the origin of the phrase **control stack**.

## Shu-Stream and Jing-River begin to act like phase shifters

At the Shu-Stream and Jing-River levels, the dominant role changes markedly.

About **58.3%** of each group falls into phase-shifter positions.

If Jing-Well points are interpreted as endpoint conditions, the positions of Shu-Stream and Jing-River look less like simple on/off gates and more like sites that alter phase relationships along the pathway.

This also helps explain why the Five-Shu points are not simply “stronger as they move upward” or “five equal divisions.”

They look more like five different levels of operational permission.

## He-Sea points enter a bridging and convergence layer

He-Sea points look different again.

Among the twelve He-Sea points, about **58.3%** have a best geometric role classified as a bridge; at the same time, about **75%** have Dampness as their primary Six-Qi label.

This combination deserves attention.

A bridge position implies transfer and convergence between segments. Dampness, in the current model, tends toward medium, aggregation, and low-potential windows. These cannot yet be equated directly, but together they give the term “sea/confluence” support from both positional and qi-transformational perspectives.

The Five-Shu points can therefore provisionally be reread as:

**Jing-Well: endpoint / node.**

**Ying-Spring: first layer away from the endpoint.**

**Shu-Stream and Jing-River: longitudinal phase shifting and transmission.**

**He-Sea: deeper bridging and convergence window.**

## A layer earlier than Five-Phase assignment

This model does not deny Five-Phase theory of the Five-Shu points.

Instead, it raises a sequencing question: perhaps the ancients first observed a recurring set of **spatial control roles**, and only later systematized them using the language of Jing, Ying, Shu, Jing, He and the Five Phases.

If so, the real value of the Five-Shu points is not merely that “this point belongs to Wood” and “that point belongs to Fire,” but that every one of the twelve channels requires a similar front-end operational interface.

That is why Five-Shu points may be especially useful as standardized anchors for cross-channel comparison.

## The missing other half: the opposite end of the pathway

Current evidence supports only the **front-end control stack**.

To demonstrate a complete two-ended boundary model, we would need to order the control points at the opposite end of each channel using the same method and test whether they form stable correspondences with the Five-Shu points.

That column is still missing from the current Master Data.

The safest present conclusion is therefore:

**The Five-Shu points are not five arbitrarily distributed representative points. Across the twelve channels, they repeatedly form a geometric control hierarchy unfolding inward from the endpoint.**
