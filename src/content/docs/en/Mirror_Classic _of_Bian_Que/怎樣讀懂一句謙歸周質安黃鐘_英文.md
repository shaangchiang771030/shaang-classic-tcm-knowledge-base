By the time we reach the “Twenty-Seven Hou,” the vocabulary built up over the previous dozens of articles is finally layered into a single sentence:

> Qian-Gui-Zhou, Zhi-An-Huangzhong; Sui-Pan-Gong, Xian-Kai-Zhi.

If treated as an ancient mnemonic to be memorized, this is extremely difficult. If unpacked layer by layer, however, it resembles a highly compressed state code.

## Layer 1: Qian-Gui — what is qi doing?

Qian and Gui come from the Nine-Qi sequence. We have already seen that Qian is related to Moyuan true qi and the Sanjiao root position, while Gui is related to inhaled clear qi and the return and convergence of qi interaction.

“Qian-Gui” therefore first tells us the transitional relation occupied by this qi-transformational event.

## Layer 2: Zhou — at which Hou is it observed?

Zhou is one of the Twenty-Seven Hou and belongs to the Cun-measure group.

This assigns Qian-Gui to a position that can be observed within the Qi-Mouth system rather than leaving it as an abstract state.

## Layer 3: Zhi-An — what tonal degree does pulse-and-breath present?

Zhi comes from the ten divisions within one tone; An comes from the ten qi-transformation tones.

Together they describe the high-low and degree-of-concord state of pulse and breath. Combinations such as “Zhi-An-Huangzhong” have already appeared in the Eight-She material.

## Layer 4: Huangzhong — which lü measure governs qi movement?

Huangzhong is one of the Twelve Lü and the sixty-fen baseline lü for the normal person. The source also connects Huangzhong with the qi-movement measure of the Taiyin system.

Thus the short phrase “Qian-Gui-Zhou, Zhi-An-Huangzhong” carries at least:

**Nine-Qi transition + Hou position + tonal division + qi-transformation tone + lü measure.**

The following phrase, “Sui-Pan-Gong, Xian-Kai-Zhi,” adds further scale, Guan-position Hou, and other tonal information, giving the same state additional observational faces.

## Why compress information this way?

If the full system contains dozens of Hou, Twelve Lü, ten tonal divisions, ten qi-transformation tones, and multiple She relations, repeatedly spelling out every dimension in ordinary prose would be extremely cumbersome.

Symbolic coding makes it possible to compress multidimensional states into short expressions that can be transmitted and consulted as a table.

Compression, however, comes at a cost: once later readers lose the codebook, only an opaque string of unfamiliar characters remains.
