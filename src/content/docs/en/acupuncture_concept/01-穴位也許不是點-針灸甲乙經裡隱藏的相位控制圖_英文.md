When we speak of “acupoints” today, it is easy to imagine a point on the surface of the body: find the location, press or needle it, and remember what it treats.

But this is precisely where the *Zhenjiu Jiayi Jing* becomes puzzling.

The same acupoint often treats symptoms that are far apart. A point on the foot can appear in passages on headache, the throat, the chest and abdomen, or even hernia and masses. If an acupoint is understood only as a local anatomical point, these distal indications are difficult to explain in one language.

We therefore changed the question: **what if an acupoint is not a “point,” but a control window at a particular phase along a channel pathway?**

## Put the twelve channels on the same ruler

The channels differ greatly in length, so directly comparing distance in centimeters has little meaning. The first step is therefore not to discuss frequency, but to normalize scale.

For each of the twelve primary channels, we provisionally take its **Jing-Well point as the starting point**, define that point as 0, and convert locations along the entire channel into relative distances from 0 to 1. These can also be expressed as phase angles from 0° to 360°.

This step matters. It allows pathways of completely different lengths—the Lung, Bladder, and Gallbladder channels, for example—to be compared using the same question:

**Where, in relative terms, is this point located with respect to the starting end of its own channel?**

In the latest dataset, all Jing-Well points of the twelve primary channels have been re-zeroed. The original model phases are retained for traceability, but formal comparison now uses the “phase measured from the Jing-Well point.”

## Phase does not have only one cycle

If a channel pathway contained only one simplest waveform, 0° to 360° would be enough to describe it.

But if second-, third-, or higher-order spatial modes can occur along the same pathway, the same acupoint will have a different local phase under different harmonic numbers *n*.

This is why the study does not force each channel to have one unique *n*. The actual data show harmonic preferences by channel—for example, some channels more often fall around n5, n6, or n10—but no channel is completely dominated by one single *n*.

A more reasonable interpretation is: **each channel resembles a waveguide capable of supporting multiple modes, while different acupoints occupy different control positions within those modes.**

This remains a model. It does not prove that the human body contains a particular physical standing wave. What can be tested is whether this geometric language predicts the locations of indications in the *Jiayi Jing* better than random relations do.

## From “where the acupoint is” to “what region can it control?”

To avoid circular reasoning, we did not simply encode “this point treats headache” as “the phase of headache equals the phase of this point.”

Formal validation uses only spatial descriptions that can be independently localized from the symptom itself, such as:

- vertex, forehead, and brain;
- throat, neck, and nape;
- shoulder, axilla, elbow, and arm;
- chest, breast, Lung, and Heart;
- epigastrium, middle burner, abdomen, and umbilicus;
- lower abdomen and genital region;
- knee, ankle, toes, and so on.

Within the existing twelve-channel dataset, we organized **625 unique “acupoint–specific symptom” pairs** that can enter formal geometric testing. Even if the same point and symptom recur in several passages, the pair is counted only once, preventing high-frequency terms from being repeatedly overweighted.

The question then becomes very concrete:

> Given an acupoint’s phase and harmonic position, does the control domain predicted by the geometric rules actually contain the symptom location recorded in the *Jiayi Jing*?

This is more than translating ancient text into modern terminology. It is a question that can be counted and falsified.

## The first surprise: acupoints look more like “windows” than “targets”

When hundreds of acupoints are placed into the same phase framework, the most notable phenomenon is not that “every point has its own unique rule,” but that the same geometric relations recur again and again.

Some points are in phase with distant positions; some preserve relative phase after crossing a node; some fall at full-length mirror positions; a smaller group of symptoms lies especially close to wave peaks or anti-peaks.

This begins to change the meaning of an acupoint.

An acupoint may not be a “target” corresponding to one organ or one disease. It may instead be **a control window along a spatially periodic pathway, capable of modifying a set of phase relations.**

If this direction is correct, the ancient phenomenon of “one point, many indications” need not be treated as dozens of unrelated empirical facts. It may represent multiple clinical manifestations of the same control position under different pathomechanistic conditions.

## What can the model currently claim?

At present, it is best at explaining **which locations may form control relationships with which other locations**. Phase alone still cannot tell us which exact symptom a patient will necessarily develop.

This limitation is important.

Phase and geometry are more like a skeleton: they are good at describing spatial reachability. Whether the final manifestation is pain, heat, cold, cough, vomiting, dizziness, or a change in mental state may still depend on intermediate layers such as the channels, Six Qi, and pathomechanism.

This series therefore does not treat “standing wave” as a proven answer. It treats it as a high-compression hypothesis that can be tested passage by passage.
