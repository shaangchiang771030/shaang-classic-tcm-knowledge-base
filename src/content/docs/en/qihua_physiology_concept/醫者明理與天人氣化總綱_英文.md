## Introduction and Core Definition
A physician does not merely treat disease names, but understands principles, knows temporal conditions and movement-qi, manages deficiency/excess and favorable/adverse dynamics, and assesses severity when determining dosage.

This concept, in the “medical principles and physiology” portion of *Yixue Tanyuan* (*Medical Exploration of Origins*), is used primarily to explain that the human body is not an isolated machine, but a system of qi transformation that receives and participates in the Yin-Yang, Five-Phase, Six-Qi, and channel movements of Heaven and Earth. If it is treated merely as a table of terms, students easily end up memorizing fragments. When it is restored to a pathway of qi transformation, however, its bridging role among diagnosis, formulas, and medicinals becomes visible.

## Classical Basis and Medical-Theory Translation
This article draws on: **Introduction**.

In plain-language terms, this concept is meant to answer three questions: first, which qi is moving in Heaven and Earth or in the body; second, where that qi comes from and where it goes; and third, when its movement becomes abnormal, what observable symptom, rhythm, or pathomechanism results. Framed this way, Yin and Yang, clear and turbid, zang-fu, channels and collaterals, and the Six Qi in the classical texts are no longer merely abstract symbols, but model variables that can be taught, recorded, and analyzed.

## Connection with the Three Parts of the Study of Heaven and Humanity
This article carries the first-principles framework of the Spacetime series into the physician's general premises for diagnosis and therapeutic method.

These thirty articles in the **Qi-Transformation Physiology** series are therefore best placed **after the Spacetime series and before the Classical Formulas series**. The Spacetime series supplies axioms about Heaven and Earth; Qi-Transformation Physiology explains how the human body receives them; the Classical Formulas series then converts pathomechanism into formulas; and the Materia Medica series connects the nature, taste, form, name, and inherited endowment of individual medicinals to the same system.

## Modern Observation and Teaching Notes
Symptom diaries, weather, sleep, heart rate, and body temperature can be placed on the same timeline to train students to see how disease trajectories interact with the environment.

In teaching, each article should ask the student to complete a small exercise with four columns: **observable indicators**, **possible symptoms**, **temporal phase**, and **body level/location**. This prevents the material from becoming mere memorization and gradually builds the ability to reason from time and space to the body, from the body to pathomechanism, and from pathomechanism to formulas and medicinals.

## Editorial and Review Focus
This article should retain its orientation toward **medical theory and physiology** rather than becoming direct treatment advice. When formulas, medicinals, or symptoms are mentioned, they should be presented for teaching and knowledge organization, so that the public is not led to think that self-diagnosis or self-medication is appropriate. The page may include an Aside noting that this series is intended for the study of Chinese medical theory and that clinical decisions still require a qualified clinician.
