Defensive qi and nutritive qi do not simply travel side by side along the same route. Defensive qi is described more like a system that switches between external and internal states with the day–night cycle: during the day it circulates twenty-five times through the Yang channels; at night it enters the Yin zang and circulates twenty-five times. At dawn it emerges from Yin into Yang and the person wakes; at sunset it moves from Yang into Yin and the person sleeps. If defensive qi cannot smoothly enter Yin from Yang, difficulty sleeping may result.

Thus the phrase **“when it emerges into Yang one wakes; when it enters Yin one sleeps”** interprets sleep as a periodic whole-body routing change of qi rather than as an isolated event in the brain alone.

Clinical use of this model must remain flexible. The key external reference is local sunrise and sunset. Across the year, daylight duration changes, so the timing and route emphasis of defensive qi would also be expected to vary within the traditional model.

If one plots the theoretical amount of daylight over the year, the curve again looks Taiji-like. The reason is straightforward: it derives from the Earth’s orbit around the Sun and the changing relation of the local horizon to sunlight.

From this defensive-qi model, behaviors likely to disturb nighttime sleep are easy to infer. The basic principle is: **do not spend daytime behaving as though it were night, or nighttime behaving as though it were day.** If you drink coffee, finish it before noon; avoid using daytime to “make up” large amounts of sleep; allow the activity of defensive qi to stay synchronized with the local Sun. When the Sun sets, defensive qi can gradually enter Yin, which may help the transition into sleep. By the same logic, Edison’s invention of electric lighting was extraordinary, but artificial light also gives human beings the ability to disturb the natural light–dark synchronization assumed by this model.

## Original Passage from *Exploring the Sources of Medicine*

“Defensive qi travels through the Yang channels twenty-five circuits by day and through the Yin zang twenty-five circuits by night. Its movement normally begins at dawn in the Yin hour from Jingming of Foot Taiyang. At dawn Yang qi emerges at the eyes; when the eyes open, qi ascends to the head … this occurs for twenty-five circuits. At sunset Yang is exhausted and Yin receives qi; it then enters the Yin zang. In entering Yin it normally follows the Foot Shaoyin channel and pours into the Kidney, then Heart, Lung, Liver, Spleen, and returns to the Kidney—this is one circuit. This also occurs twenty-five times. At dawn Yin is exhausted and Yang receives qi, so it again emerges into the Yang channels. … When defensive qi enters Yin, one sleeps; when it emerges into Yang, one wakes. One day contains one hundred *ke*; it circulates fifty times around the body. This is the measure of defensive qi. The *Nan Jing* phrase that nutritive and defensive qi ‘follow one another’ means that nutritive qi travels within the vessels and defensive qi outside them, mutually accompanying one another; it does not mean that they travel together through the same channel.”
