After calculating one whole-body circuit, the *Mirror Classic of Bian Que* does something characteristic of ancient medicine: it does not leave the body enclosed within itself, but immediately compares that circuit with the day.

The text lists the twenty-eight lunar lodges, one hundred ke, twelve double-hours, twenty-four steps, and the number of day-and-night circuits of Ying and Wei, then says:

> Through one day and one night it proceeds through the twelve double-hours and twenty-four steps, while the water clock measures one hundred ke. When qi completes its course, it has circulated throughout the body and accords with the Way of Heaven; therefore this is called normal. The normal is the standard of compass, square, weight, and balance.

This reveals a second layer of what “normal” means.

## Bodily normality is not merely normality at one instant

Earlier, one breath had a baseline proportion. But if the body is a dynamic system operating over long periods, a normal single breath does not guarantee that the rhythm of the whole day is normal.

The *Mirror Classic* therefore expands the temporal scale:

- one breath is a short cycle;
- many breaths form a larger unit;
- many units form a circuit;
- many circuits are placed within day and night;
- day and night are compared with the twelve double-hours, one hundred ke, and the movement of the day.

This is a concept of **nested cycles**. Small cycles exist within larger ones, while larger cycles provide a phase background for smaller cycles.

## “Accord with the Way of Heaven” should first be understood as cyclical correspondence

There is no need to begin with a mystical explanation.

Ancient observers could directly see day and night, shadows, water-clock time, sleep and waking, food intake, breathing, and activity. Placing bodily rhythms within the daily cycle is a natural observational move.

What distinguishes the *Mirror Classic* is that it seeks to quantify this correspondence rather than stop with the statement that “human beings correspond with Heaven and Earth.” It repeatedly uses countable units such as circuit, step, ke, division, and breath.

## What role does “fifty Ying” play?

The text says that Ying and Wei circulate through the body “fifty circuits plus a remainder” over day and night; the later discussion of the twenty-seven Hou again refers to “qi of Hou and She traveling through fifty Ying circuits.” Thus fifty Ying is not an isolated term. It is a common background connecting daily circulation, channel flow, and Hou-She observation.

For now it can be understood as **a total-cycle scale for the body’s qi movement across a day**.

How the exact numbers should be converted into the modern twenty-four-hour day is a later textual and experimental question. What matters internally is the hierarchy of cycles.

## “Normal” is a synchronized state

Translated into somewhat modern conceptual language, the *Mirror Classic*’s “normal” is not merely a value lying within a normal range. It is closer to multiple cycles maintaining appropriate proportions and phases.

Breathing, pulse movement, channel progression, and day-night rhythm operate on different time scales. The author attempts to connect them within a common numerical framework.

This helps explain why later pathology pays such attention to “correct position,” “lost position,” “shift,” and “return.” In a cyclical system, the problem may not simply be too much or too little; it may also be **occurring at the wrong temporal position**.
