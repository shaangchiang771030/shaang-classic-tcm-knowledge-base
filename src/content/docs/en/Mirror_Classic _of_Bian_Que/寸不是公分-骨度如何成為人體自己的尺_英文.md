When modern readers encounter “qi travels six cun in one breath,” the first instinct is often to ask: how many centimeters is six cun?

But the *Mirror Classic of Bian Que* itself warns that this is not the best first question.

It says:

> Cun is a proportional ruler based on bone measure. The pulse travels six cun in one breath; the movement of breath is a proportional ruler.

The key words are “proportional ruler.”

## Bone measure is a scale defined by the person’s own body

People differ in height, limb length, and trunk proportions. If one fixed external length were imposed on every body to describe channel distances, ordinary anatomical variation could easily be mistaken for differences in qi movement.

The *Mirror Classic* instead establishes a relative scale from the person’s own bone measure. “Six cun” therefore first means **six divisions within an individualized body-proportion scale**, rather than a fixed metric length.

The later “Kuidu” chapter emphasizes this again:

> People are tall or short; take the divisions of bone measure and use them proportionally as a ruler.

On this basis it lists proportional lengths for the six hand-yang, six hand-yin, six foot-yang, six foot-yin routes and for the Chong, Ren, Du, Qiao, Wei, and Dai vessels. Bone measure is therefore not a casual remark near the beginning; it is the geometric foundation of the book.

## Why does this design matter?

The *Mirror Classic* needs to compare two things at once:

First, different human bodies; second, different channels and times within the same body.

If the unit of length is not individualized first, it becomes difficult to distinguish whether an observed difference comes from body size or from pulse and breath.

The proportional bone ruler can therefore be understood as a form of **scale normalization**: first define what “one ruler” means for this person, then ask how many divisions qi travels in that person.

## The position of the skin regions matters too

When introducing bone measure, the text also says:

> What can be measured are the divisions of bone measure and the positions of the skin regions.

Measurement therefore includes not only length but also **position**. The same pulse event has different significance depending on what bodily division it appears in, whether it lies anterior or posterior, above or below. These distinctions later enter the systems of Hou, She, and the Qi Mouth.

In other words, the *Mirror Classic* has at least two kinds of coordinate:

- bone measure answers “how far?”;
- skin regions and divisions answer “where?”

And breath answers “within how much time?”

## Do not convert ancient measures into centimeters too early

This does not mean ancient people had no concept of absolute length. It means that for understanding the *Mirror Classic*’s internal algorithm, **the proportional relation takes priority over metric conversion**.

Future experiments could record a participant’s actual body length, bone-measure segments, and metric distances at the same time, then test whether the classical proportions can be reproduced. That is a validation question, not an assumption to impose while reading the text.

Seen this way, “six cun in one breath” is no longer a mysterious ancient number. It means: **within a coordinate system that uses the person’s own body as ruler and the person’s own breath as clock, define a baseline scale of qi movement.**
