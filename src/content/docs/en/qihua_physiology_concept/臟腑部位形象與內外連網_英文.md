The zang-fu should not be understood as isolated organs. *Exploring the Sources of Medicine* describes a continuous **zang-fu–channel–limb/joint network**: channel qi is rooted internally in the zang-fu and connects externally with the limbs and joints. The twelve channels are arranged in layers according to Yin and Yang, interior and exterior, front/back/side, and depth. Taiyang and Shaoyin run mainly along the posterior body; Yangming and Taiyin mainly along the anterior body; Shaoyang and Jueyin mainly along the sides. Hand and foot Yin/Yang channels also have directional routes: chest to hand, hand to head, head to foot, and foot to chest. Human spatial organization is therefore not two separate worlds—“organs inside, limbs outside”—but a three-dimensional network in which the channels connect internal zang-fu with the external body surface.

Traditional channel nomenclature itself is a remarkable compression system. A channel name can be decomposed into **[Hand/Foot] × [one of the Six Qi / Three Yin–Three Yang] × [principal originating zang or fu] × [Five-Phase property]**. For example: **[Hand] [Taiyin] [Lung] [Metal]**.

In our Acupuncture section, precise positional data for the classical acupoints has also been used to test whether a high proportion of points lie near antinodes, nodes/valleys, or quarter-wavelength positions within proposed standing-wave harmonic models. In that dataset, more than 90% of 365 points fell into one of the predefined harmonic geometries. This is treated as a research hypothesis and quantitative modeling result, not as proof that the channels are literally identical to modern physical strings.

The classical tradition also states that the six-channel systems differ in their relative abundance of Qi and Blood. Within the resonance hypothesis explored here, such differences could imply distinct characteristic modes or frequencies for these “invisible strings.” Related modern clinical investigations by physician Xu Jizong’s team, and the book *A Five-Tone System of the Yellow Emperor’s Inner Canon Based on the Fu Xing Jue*, may be consulted as additional lines of inquiry.

Following the same line of thought, references to the Five Tones scattered across different chapters of the *Inner Canon* may be interpreted as attempts to use musical resonance language to describe qi-transformative phenomena within the body. For a separate cross-domain exploration of Five Tones with the Five Zang and the Twelve Pitch-Pipes with the twelve channels, see the Bian Que Mirror Classic section.

## Original Passage from *Exploring the Sources of Medicine*

“The six zang and six fu give rise to the twelve channels. Channel qi is rooted internally in the zang-fu and connects externally with the limbs and joints. The Spleen, Kidney, Liver, Gallbladder, Stomach, and Bladder channels travel through the feet and are the six Foot channels; the Lung, Heart, Pericardium, Sanjiao, Large Intestine, and Small Intestine channels travel through the hands and are the six Hand channels. The hands have Three Yin and Three Yang, and the feet likewise have Three Yin and Three Yang. … Yang channels are on the exterior and Yin channels on the interior. Taiyang is outermost, in the division of skin and body hair; next comes Yangming, then Shaoyang, then Taiyin, then Shaoyin, then Jueyin, which lies closest to the bones. Yang channels belong to the fu and connect with the zang; Yin channels belong to the zang and connect with the fu. … Among the Yang channels, Yangming lies anteriorly, Shaoyang in the middle, and Taiyang posteriorly; among the Yin channels, Taiyin lies anteriorly, Jueyin in the middle, and Shaoyin posteriorly. The Hand Yin channels go from the chest to the hand; the Yang channels from the hand to the head; the Foot Yang channels from the head to the foot; the Yin channels from the foot to the chest. … Taiyang and Shaoyin travel along the back of the body; Yangming and Taiyin along the front; Shaoyang and Jueyin along the sides. These are the regional arrangements of the channels.”
