When Chinese medical time models—such as the expected relief times of the Six-Channel disorders or the Five Movements and Six Qi—are used in clinical diagnosis or prescription, many people encounter situations in which the model seems to fail. This is often attributed here not to the model itself but to neglect of the boundaries defined by the **correct calendar and time scale**. In any temporal model, alignment of the **origin point** and the definition of time-zone boundaries are decisive; otherwise, a tiny initial discrepancy can lead to a vast error. In basic physical terms, this can be understood as a phase difference in an energy fluctuation that has not been captured precisely.

The central principle is **coordinate alignment and boundary effects**. Time is continuous, but humans divide it into scales—such as solar terms and double-hours—to package recurring regularities. Different models correspond to different underlying physical processes, so their temporal origins can also differ. If coordinate systems are mixed arbitrarily—for example, confusing the astronomical winter-solstice point with the meteorological Great Cold point—the substitutions into the model will break down.
For example, the hottest and coldest days of a year do not occur exactly on the summer and winter solstices. Because the Earth itself acts as a thermal reservoir, solar-radiation intensity and ambient temperature naturally exhibit a phase lag between these two physical quantities.
