To quantify changes in the body, the first task is not to measure length but to answer: **what unit of time should be used?**

The *Mirror Classic of Bian Que* chooses neither the water clock nor the modern second. It chooses a physiological cycle that is continuously occurring within every person—breathing.

The text says:

> One exhalation and one inhalation make one breath; intercalated with a great breath, this is called one breath of a normal person.

This sentence is important. It does not merely mean “take one breath.” It establishes a complete cycle used during examination.

## One breath is the body’s own clock

Exhalation and inhalation are movements in opposite directions. Exhalation moves outward and inhalation inward; only when one exit and one entry are completed is the basic cycle complete.

If only exhalation or only inhalation is observed, only half a cycle is seen. Joining the two allows one to compare how many pulse movements occur within a complete cycle, how far qi travels, and in what sequence the pulse movements appear.

This is why almost all later quantitative descriptions return to “one breath”:

> In one exhalation the pulse moves twice and qi travels three cun; in one inhalation the pulse likewise moves twice and qi travels three cun. When exhalation and inhalation settle, the pulse has moved five times and qi has traveled six cun; this is called a normal person.

Three quantities are placed in one coordinate system: **respiratory phase, number of pulse movements, and distance traveled by qi.**

## Why are “settled breath” and “great breath” also included?

The text does not assume physiology behaves like a mechanical pendulum. There are transitions between inhalation and exhalation, and the pulse does not stop when one breath ends. It therefore explicitly includes the “settling of the breath” and the “great breath” within the cycle.

This makes “one breath of a normal person” more like a **cycle window for observing a complete physiological rhythm** than a simple conversion of respiratory rate into seconds.

Later, when the text discusses different human types, it describes long breath, gradual breath, rapid breath, and normal patterns of four, five, or six pulse movements in one breath. This further reminds us that the baseline breath is first a **standard reference**, not a claim that every person must be mechanically identical.

## Why not use an external clock?

Another feature of the *Mirror Classic* is its emphasis on proportional comparison.

People differ in height, so length is measured by proportional bone measure. People also differ in breathing rhythm, so time is first referenced to their own breath. The advantage is that individual scale can be normalized before comparing different locations and phases within the same person.

A modern reader can understand this as a methodological choice without immediately claiming equivalence to any specific modern measurement technique: **first identify a stable, repeating cycle intrinsic to the body, use it as the baseline, and then examine how other signals change relative to that cycle.**

## “Normal” is not an average; it is the standard of rule, compass, weight, and balance

The *Mirror Classic* later says: “The normal is the standard of compass, square, weight, and balance.”

Here, “normal” is not a vague impression of health. It is the reference by which comparison becomes possible. Only after a normal rhythm is established can one discuss fast and slow, long and short, correct and displaced position, flourishing and decline, conformity and reversal.

Thus one breath is the first genuine unit of measurement in the book.
