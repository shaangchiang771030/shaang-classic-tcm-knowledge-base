### If Acupoints Are Not Isolated Points, How Should We Rethink the Channel Network?

The most familiar way to study acupuncture points today is to associate each point with a list of indications: which points treat headache, which treat stomach pain, which treat low-back pain. This is clinically convenient, but it leaves a theoretical gap. Why this location rather than another? Why can a point on the foot affect the head, throat, or internal organs? And why do the Jing-Well, Ying-Spring, Shu-Stream, Jing-River, and He-Sea points form such a stable sequence?

This series on phase geometry in the *Zhenjiu Jiayi Jing* addresses that gap. It tries to place acupoints back into the geometry of an entire channel—and ultimately the network of the Twelve Channels—instead of treating them as isolated therapeutic dots. The primary question therefore changes from “What does this point treat?” to “Where is this point on the channel, what structural role does that position play, and could the recorded indications be clinical projections of that structural role?”

[Acupoints May Not Be 'Points': A Hidden Phase-Control Map in the Zhenjiu Jiayi Jing](/en/acupuncture_concept/01-穴位也許不是點-針灸甲乙經裡隱藏的相位控制圖/) must therefore come first. Its purpose is not to prove a modern physical model, but to change the unit of observation: a channel becomes a path whose relative positions can be compared. By taking the Jing-Well point as a shared origin for each channel, channels of different absolute lengths can be placed into a common relative coordinate system. “Phase” should first be understood in this modest geometric sense of position along a path rather than immediately being made mystical.

Once a coordinate exists, [Why Can Three Geometric Rules Recover Nearly 90% of Jiayi Jing Acupoint Indications?](/en/acupuncture_concept/02-三條幾何語法-為什麼能回溯近九成甲乙經穴位主治/) can ask whether position alone predicts recurring functional roles. Nodes, peaks, bridges, cross-line couplings, and related geometric rules are not intended to replace the *Jiayi Jing* text; they are hypotheses about whether hundreds of recorded indications may be generated by a smaller set of structural rules. The first article establishes coordinates; the second begins to search for grammar.

A rule has little value if it cannot explain clinically difficult cases. [Why Can a Point on the Foot Treat the Head and Throat? A Geometric Answer to Distal Point Selection](/en/acupuncture_concept/03-一個腳上的穴-為什麼會治頭與咽喉-遠端取穴的幾何答案/) therefore turns to distal point selection—the familiar but structurally puzzling fact that a point on the foot can treat the head or throat. With the coordinate system and geometric grammar already established, the point need not be imagined as “remote-controlling” another organ. Its importance may instead arise from being located at a boundary, transition, bridge, or cross-network coupling position. This article is not a fourth rule; it is the first concrete clinical test of the preceding abstract structure.

If position carries structural meaning, a natural next question follows: why did the classical tradition repeatedly single out the five categories Jing, Ying, Shu, Jing, and He? [Jing-Well, Ying-Spring, Shu-Stream, Jing-River, and He-Sea May Be a Boundary Control Stack](/en/acupuncture_concept/04-井滎輸經合不是五個古老名詞-它們可能是一套邊界控制棧/) should therefore not be read too early. Only after channel geometry has been established can the Five-Shu system be reconsidered as a standardized control architecture in which positions near the channel front-end progressively alter depth and scope of intervention.

At that point the model looks elegant—but serious research cannot select only successful examples. [Are the Six Qi Really Written into Channel Phase? Wind, Cold, Dampness, Fire Windows—and an Unexpected Gap](/en/acupuncture_concept/05-六氣真的寫在經絡相位上嗎-風寒濕火窗口與一個意外漏洞/) deliberately stress-tests it. If channel phase really carries differentiation of pathomechanism, do indications associated with wind, cold, dampness, and fire cluster in reproducible phase windows? Some do, while dryness and summerheat do not appear as expected. That failure is valuable because it forces us to distinguish the classification used in the classical source, later Six-Excess labels, and the Six Qi of Five-Movements-and-Six-Qi theory. A strong model must expose its boundaries rather than erase its counterexamples.

Finally, [Why Is Fengchi Called 'Wind Pool'? Acupoint Names May Be Compressed Codes Rather Than Poetry](/en/acupuncture_concept/06-風池為什麼叫風池-古人穴名可能不是詩意而是壓縮碼/) introduces an independent evidence stream: acupoint names. If earlier geometric rules were inferred mainly from location and indication, names provide another source that can be checked independently. Roots such as wind, pool, sea, spring, stream, valley, gate, pass, palace, and storehouse can be examined to ask whether they merely form poetic imagery or compress information about qi movement, convergence, entry-exit, and structural function. Not every name yields a neat explanation—and precisely for that reason, the roots that show stable cross-point associations become more informative.

The six articles therefore form a deliberate cognitive curve: **hypothesis → rules → clinical case → control structure → stress test → independent validation**. The reader should finish not with six more facts to memorize, but with a new way to read the *Jiayi Jing*. An acupoint is no longer only “what it treats”; it is also a relative position on a path, a structural relation to other points, a possible recurring control role, and an object whose name, indication, and pathomechanism can be cross-validated.

That is the perspective this project tries to recover: acupoints may not be hundreds of isolated points scattered across the body, but intervention windows located at structurally important positions in a larger channel network.
